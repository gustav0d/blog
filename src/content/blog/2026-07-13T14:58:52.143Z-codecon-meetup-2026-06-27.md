On June 27th this year I went to a Codecon Meetup in Belo Horizonte. It was the first meetup I've been to. As far as that day, I honestly didn't know for sure what to expect and if it was worth it or not.

I wanted to go at first because 1) I've never been to one; and 2) I wanted to meet the two guys giving the talk (I had just seen them on my x/twitter feed so I thought it would be nice actually seeing if they're actually real or not).

There were two talks:

- Using AI to Scale Processes in a Startup, by [Sam](https://x.com/samsantosb)
- The bug is not in the prompt, by [Guilherme Ananias](https://x.com/noghartt) ([noghartt](https://noghartt.dev/))

## The knowledge from the talks

Sam's talk approached some concepts on how he and the team he's in use AI in their daily workflow, explained some do's and don't's when it comes to giving the context to AI, in simple terms:

- do not overflow your `{CLAUDE, AGENTS}.md`; instead:
  - instruct the AI agent where to look for the right information per module, use _guidelines_
- guidelines
  - divide the knowledge of your application into proper documentation where the AI agents are able to grasp the necessary information and not to overflow the agent's context
- don't ship more than 400-line PRs (not counting tests and documentation):
  - you're likely not going to review the PR; and
  - if you do review it, it's likely not going to be a thorough review; and
  - you'll over-rely on AI reviews
- the scale part – prepare for async-like work with the AI
  - connect your agent to slack
  - use AI code reviewers in PRs, such as Kodus, Coderabbit; connect them with your own guidelines
  - do your work on the cloud/vm/containers - this is what leverages you to be able to move from one task to another
  - make your dev agents talk to the code reviewer and vice versa - review it properly when this process is done.

Noghartt's talk was more _systemic_-centered, if I can say so.

That means he basically turned the attention not to the _harness/context/ai_ engineering buzzwords I've been hearing a lot lately in social media, but to _why_ failure was allowed in the first place.

This mindset – I believe – is really what makes great teams great: if a bug is shipped to production, it doesn't matter who "shipped" it – or who instructed the AI agent to do so – the fact is that the whole system failed to identify that gap that allowed failure, hence there's absolutely _no guilt_ in it, just the notion that the team must think and work together to find the structural gap in their system that allowed it in the first place. I honestly believe that is the singular mindset aspect that makes a team strive their way into building great, reliable products.

## The surprisingly best part

Even though I really liked the talks, I truly enjoyed much more what happened later.

I was able to talk to everyone, talk a bit more about myself, listen to them in a non-lecture-talk, non-social-media way – in a more _humanized way_ if I can say so.

After the meetup was over some people got together and we went to a restaurant and had lunch. And oh my. That was definitely the part I learned the most. It's incredible how many topics per second are introduced, explained, presented trade-offs.

Really. It goes from what [OLAP](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/olap) is, how awesome [TigerBeetle](https://tigerbeetle.com/) is, tech books recommendations, to Bolha Dev's (Brazillian x/twitter sort of community) gossip. Truly great stuff.

As I was heading home I just thought how nice it would be if I went to more meetups, more conferences, more events more often. How inspiring it can be on a daily basis, not just on the technical side of things – which is truly great btw, lots of interesting things to learn there – but on the human side as well: create real connections (not just LinkedIn stuff), talk about tech and non-tech stuff you like.

It was worth it.
